Tracie tells me she was twelve the first time she went to the Country.

She met some kids at Travis Park who knew how to get in. They took her down through the river, around the back, and jumped the wall. Sam worked security and knew she was underage. When she caught Tracie on the dance floor, she would pull her outside, take the ID, and cut it up.
Tracie would get another.

Sam would cut that one too. 

I want to ask Tracie how high the wall was, but she’s already talking about the acid. She would take it and wait for the dance floor to change. She remembers the lights, the music, her first mother. Years later she would work behind the bar herself. 

I picture Sam finding her again. Tracie sees her first, maybe. She turns away and Sam has to push through the crowd to get to her. I imagine that Sam could have recognized her anywhere, that after enough nights she could find Tracie by the back of her head, by the way she danced. 

I start looking for the wall. 

Of everything she remembers, this is what I want to see. 
I look through photographs of the Country trying to find it, but none are especially helpful. There’s a stone house with tall ceilings, living room furniture, a rose window, mirrors reflecting neon, a disco ball. Before it was the Country it was the Old Heidelberg Restaurant. Someone’s house before that. 

The club was at 1122 North St. Mary’s. The dance floor had moving lights and fog machines. I keep looking at the photographs, trying to work out which direction the river is. 

There were speakers large enough for Joe Trevino Jr. to climb on top of them, “Once or twice.” He wore ragged white Daisy Dukes, a white tank top, and a construction hat. A Village People costume. I imagine the backs of his thighs against the speaker box, the bass traveling up his legs. He remembers boyfriends too, but puts the word in quotation marks.

*“Boyfriends.”*

Richard Jerry Ramos was eighteen. Fiesta Week, 1977. A coworker brought him to the Country for the first time, and when they reached the main dance floor, Marvin Gaye was playing. 

“Got to Give It Up.” 

I play it. 

The song starts with a party already happening. Voices. Someone yelling from across the room and people talking over one another. There’s laughter and the drums come up under them. Richard walked in and saw men dancing with men. He remembers being nervous, and that ten minutes later he wasn’t. He said it felt like entering another world. 

I listen to it twice, twelve minutes in all. 

I’m trying to imagine those first ten minutes, but I keep thinking about the eleventh. I wonder if he was dancing by then, or if he stood there watching until someone took his hand. 

Hap didn’t let microphones in the booth. Jamie Alexander, the Country’s first regular DJ, remembered Hap explaining it to him. He didn’t want the police taking the mic and ordering everybody around during the raids. Jamie was told to turn the music up if they came in. 

The military had also decided servicemen weren't supposed to be there. Since the Country was an establishment that "catered to homosexuals," soldiers came looking for men who belonged to them.

There are photographs from the Country where almost everyone seems to be touching somebody. Hands on shoulders. Arms around waists. Bodies crowded around little tables with drinks between them. It would have been easy to get lost. 

Joyce Felter remembers military police coming around and writing down license plates in the parking lot at an earlier location of the Country outside town. She says they had a habit of investigating one's shoes. Shiny dress shoes could give away a serviceman. If somebody arrived in the wrong shoes, they’d pass over spare cowboy boots to put on instead. 

I like to think about this. Someone whistles and the music gets louder. Bodies spin away from one another. Men find a woman and her woman finds a man. Someone jumps into the boots. I wonder whether they had enough sizes, whether anybody complained about the fit.

Paul remembers the motel. Twenty dollars a night, Joe says. Across the river was Valero where employees arriving for work could catch a glimpse of the bar's prior patrons having sex in the motel pool. 

Valero wanted the land for parking. Eventually, they got it. 

I go there on a Wednesday. 

It’s 4:52 in the afternoon and the parking lot is almost empty. I must admit I expected more, but at least it’s free. The river is still there of course, below the lot. There's a small beaten path covered in crushed pink flowers that winds down toward the water, tucked away in the corner. A chain necklace hangs over a fence that's bent halfway down, holding up a NO TRESPASSING sign.

I think about Tracie. 

I look at the path and try to decide if this could have been the way she came. I don't know what the river looked like then, where the wall stood, whether she had to get her shoes wet. She doesn't mention her shoes but I think I would have asked about that too.

Later I find photographs online of people using the parking lot to watch the Pride parade. They sit in the open trunks of their cars, looking out toward the celebration. I like that they chose this spot. I wonder how many know what used to be here. 

Pieces salvaged from the Country’s demolition were taken to Hap's house on River Road and made into a wall. The rose window survived too. 

And there’s her wall.

Hap went on to open the Bonham exchange in 1981. I’ve been there many times. 

I think of myself there, sitting in my boyfriend's lap at the picnic tables on the balcony, sharing a cigarette. The smoke blows into my eyes and I have to look down to blink it out. I smile at the circle of cowboy boots. It’s crowded and loud and I love it that way. 

I think about Richard walking into the Country for the first time, how long those ten minutes must have felt. I try to remember the first time I went to Bonham, but I can’t remember what song was playing. I can't even remember who I went with. I can remember nights that have blurred into each other so completely that I couldn't tell you which happened first. I remember being pressed against someone on a dance floor and feeling the sweat on the back of their neck.

I think about the photographs of the Country. The arms around waists. The men leaning together at the tables. Joe on the speakers. Richard walking in and watching everybody dance. I keep trying to make the rooms fit inside each other. I wonder if there was a night when somebody stood at the Country and felt exactly as I have felt at the Bonham, so happy they were almost sick with it.

No one has come in to turn off the music.

I wonder if Richard ever went back to the Country and found it ordinary. If after a while he stopped being astonished to see men dancing with men. If he got bored with the music one night and went outside for some air. If he ever stood there waiting for somebody who was late and wished he'd stayed home. I hope he did. I hope he got to have so many nights there that some of them were terrible.

I want to ask him if he remembers the last time he went. Whether he knew it would be the last. I want to ask Tracie the same thing. Whether she went back after Sam stopped cutting up her IDs. Whether she remembers the last song she heard there.

I wonder if either of them would remember me, had I been there, which feels silly considering I was born decades later. I still find myself wanting to be somewhere in those photographs, even at the edge of one, even with my face turned away.

James tells me about Larry almost as an afterthought. Larry Dillard had been a DJ at the Country. Before it closed, James says, Larry buried a time capsule there. 
He doesn't know where.

I ask what's inside.

He doesn't know that either.

I want the time capsule to be there. This is different from believing that it is. I want Larry to have chosen a place nobody would find before the building came down. I want him to have dug deeper than he needed to. I want something ordinary inside. A photograph. A matchbook. Handwriting. One of those little wooden cocktail sticks with an umbrella.

Under the parking lot, maybe, there is still a box. I want there to be something more than concrete.
