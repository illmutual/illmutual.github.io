I wait for tomorrow to tell me what we’re doing. Usually it does. Tomorrow has never been particularly creative. Sometimes there is a knock at the door, or a text from an old friend, or someone stops me in the street to ask if I have a minute. I always have a minute. I’ve had years this way. 

I have never known when something was starting. Once I worked at a bakery for eleven months because I went inside to get out of the rain and the man behind the counter asked if I knew how to make coffee. I said yes, which was not true, but he showed me anyway and after that it was. When he asked me to come back the next morning, I did. My name was on the schedule. 

I got used to waking up before the sun and coming home smelling sweet and burnt. On Tuesdays and Thursdays I worked with a woman who stole strawberries from the walk-in and ate them over the sink. When she asked if I wanted one, I said yes. 

Her name was Bellamy. She had black hair and always remembered to bring a jacket. The first time she came to my apartment, she asked if she could take her shoes off and where she should put them. I said wherever. 

I own seven pairs of shoes and I wear two of them. One pair is for rain, but I don’t count those. There are black heels I bought for a wedding and haven’t worn since, a pair of boots that make my feet look too big, and some old sneakers I keep meaning to throw out. I mostly wear a pair of white sneakers. When they wear out I buy the same pair again. 


I go to the grocery store almost every day. I know this is inefficient but I don’t understand how people know on Sunday what they will want to eat on Wednesday. Sometimes I buy six apples and spend the rest of the week resenting them.

There is an open suitcase on my bedroom floor that I keep tripping over. I have put three shirts in it. My oldest friend calls me when she drives because it makes her less anxious, and I can always tell when she gets on the highway because she stops finishing her sentences. We’ve known each other for seventeen years. She has a scar on her knee from when we were six. I don’t remember how it happened but she says I was there. 

Today she is telling me about a woman at work who microwaves tuna in the break room. I tell her this should be punishable by death, and she says that’s exactly what she thought. She tells me something else about the woman, but a truck passes her and I can’t hear it. I don’t ask her to repeat herself. A few minutes later neither of us are talking. I can hear her turn signal. 

“Still there?” I ask. 

“Yeah.” 

She asks what I’m doing. I look at the suitcase. I tell her I'm cleaning. 

Eventually she gets where she’s going and we say goodbye. I lie down on the floor beside the suitcase until the carpet leaves little red marks on my cheek. From here I can see under the bed: a bottle cap, two hair clips, and something I hope is dust. My arm goes numb. I let it. My stomach growls and I wait to see if it happens again. It does. There’s half a sandwich in the fridge and I eat it standing in front of the open door. My phone buzzes in the other room. I finish the sandwich before I go look. 

By the end of my first month at the bakery I was practicing my latte art. I had to practice for many more months. I learned which customers tipped and which ones wanted to talk before seven in the morning. The old man who ordered a plain bagel every day called me sweetheart. I hated that. Bellamy said I should charge him extra. I did from then on. Fifty cents. He never noticed, and I put the fifty cents in the tip jar. Bellamy said that defeated the point. 

I take another shirt out of the closet, fold it over, and toss it on the bed. I take out two more. There are four notifications on my phone: two emails, a spam risk sent to voicemail, and a text asking what time I’ll be there. I type six, delete it, seven thirty. The weather app says it’s supposed to rain tonight and I have never bought an umbrella. I’ve had plenty, though. My last one was clear with a broken spoke that caught my hair every time I closed it. Someone left it at the bar across the street from the bakery. They gave you free fries on Wednesdays. You still had to buy a drink and that seemed fair. The fries weren’t very good but we went anyway. We usually got there by five. I liked the bar better before it got crowded. Around eight they turned up the music and people started having to lean across the tables to hear each other. Eventually Bellamy would move her chair around to my side, or we’d give up and move to the smoking lounge. It was colder up there than the rest of the bar. There was a vent in the ceiling that moved the air around but never seemed to take the smoke with it. One night Bellamy asked me what the first thing I remembered was. 

“I think I was lying down,” I said.

“Where?”

“On the floor.” 

“I gathered that.”

I tried to remember. I was on my back underneath a dining room table. Someone had stuck a piece of gum to the bottom. I touched it with one finger. It was hard around the edges and still soft in the middle.

“Whose table was it?” Bellamy asked. 

“I don’t know.” 

“Your house?”

“Maybe.” 

“That’s not much of a memory.”

“It’s the first one I’ve got.”

“Fair.” 

“What’s yours?”

She rested her head in one hand. “I’m in a swimming pool with my dad.” 

“How old are you?”

“Three, maybe. I had this yellow bathing suit with oranges on it.”

“You remember what you were wearing?”

“I’ve seen pictures.”

“That’s cheating!”

“Oh whatever.” She thought for another second. “My dad had his hands under my back. I was floating.”

“Could you swim?”

“No. He was holding me.” 

“Oh.”

“But I couldn’t feel his hands.” 

“I remember looking up and everything sounding far away.”
