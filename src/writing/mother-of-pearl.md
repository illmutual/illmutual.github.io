She smiled, pleased with herself. I though about telling her where it had come from. I imagined her looking at the pearl again. Perhaps putting down on the table between us. 

I let her keep trying it on. 

The first one had come suddenly. I noticed the lump while eating a peach. It was beneath my tongue, slightly to the left, where the skin folds up from the bottom of the mouth. At first I though a piece of the pit had gotten stuck there. I pressed it with my tongue and felt something round and hard, buried in the soft tissue.

I looked up salivary stones. Then mouth cancer. The photographs were unpleasant, so I went back to salivary stones.

For a week I watched it grow. The skin became thin enough that I could see something white pressing against it. I took photographs every evening, though I could never get the angle right. Most were blurry, in others my tongue obscured the lump entirely.

One night, while brushing my teeth, I caught the swollen place against the back of my toothbrush.

Something moved.

I stopped brushing and touched it with one finger. The skin gave a little, and a small, hard object slipped onto my tongue.

I spat it into my palm.

There was a thin streak of blood across it. I rinsed it beneath the faucet, turning it carefully between my fingers until the water ran clear.

It was round, white, and surprisingly heavy for its size. Its surface had a faint, layered shine. Under the bathroom light, it looked almost pink.

I found a blue dish in the back of my dresser drawer. There were two safety pins and a broken earring in it, which I shook onto the bed.

I put the pearl in the middle.

The second appeared nine days later. I knew what it was immediately, though it formed farther back, where I couldn't see it. For several days, I could feel it whenever I swallowed. I began to recognize its position and the pressure it made against the surrounding tissue. I was disappointed when it finally came loose.

The third was larger, with a warm cream color and an extraordinary shine. The fourth was almost perfectly round.

I bought a gold pendant setting and made a necklace out of that one.

There was something strange about seeing it around my neck. I had held it in my mouth for nearly two weeks, felt it growing beneath my tongue, worried at it with my teeth. Now it looked like something I might have inherited from my grandmother.

I liked that.

I liked the pearls themselves, too, although less than I liked knowing where they came from. Sometimes I held one in my mouth again, turning it over with my tongue. It was never the same. It rolled around uselessly, too smooth to catch against anything.

It had been a few weeks when I decided they must've stopped. 

At first, I was relieved. The little cuts beneath my tongue healed. I could eat without having to move food around the tender places in my mouth. I bought a bag of chips and finished it in one sitting.

After three weeks, I began checking beneath my tongue in the mornings.

After five, I made a dentist's appointment.

He examined me with a little mirror and said everything looked healthy. Whatever obstruction I'd had appeared to have resolved itself.

"That's good," he said.

I asked whether it might happen again.

"Hopefully not."

I had brought the smallest pearl with me, wrapped in a tissue in my purse. I'd wanted to show it to him, but he'd already begun talking about sending a sample away. 

At home, I took the necklace off and put it back in the dish.

I began reading about pearls. I learned about nacre, about the tissue that produces it, and the way an oyster can spend years coating something foreign in successive layers. I bought a book with photographs of oysters cut open to reveal their soft interiors.

In one, the flesh had been peeled back to expose a pearl almost as large as the animal itself. I looked at that photograph often.

I wondered what it had felt like before the pearl was large enough to be seen.

At night, I lay in bed with my tongue pressed against the roof of my mouth, searching for the old swellings. The scars were almost impossible to find.

Sometimes I dreamed that my mouth was full of pearls. They knocked against my teeth when I spoke, and I had to keep swallowing to prevent them from spilling out. Once, I woke with my fingers in my mouth.

I began to resent the five in the dish. They were finished. I knew each one by its shape and color, and they no longer surprised me. I could arrange them from smallest to largest, hold them against the light, turn them over a hundred times. Nothing about them would change.

Anna asked about her necklace.

I told her I was working on it.

"You don't have to, you know."

"I know."

"I feel bad now."

"Don't."

She laughed. "Okay. God."

I had four perfectly good pearls at home. I could have made her necklace that afternoon.

Instead, I went to the pet store.

The bag of aquarium sand sat beneath my bathroom sink for eleven days.

One evening, I took the sand out from beneath the sink and set it beside the book.

I was frightened by how badly I wanted to begin.

I put everything away and made dinner. I watched half a movie. I called Anna and asked if she wanted to get coffee that weekend.

The next evening, I went back into the bathroom. 

For a while, I couldn't make myself do anything. When I finally did, the pain was much worse than I had imagined. I waited for it to ease, breathing through my nose. Saliva dripped from my mouth in red strings. I kept swallowing before remembering not to, choking on wet clumps of sand. 

The book was open beside the faucet. In the photograph, the oyster's flesh was folded back, cuffed neatly and pink. I shut it.

The underside of my tongue was swollen and ragged. I thought it quite ugly. 

I began rinsing my mouth, but stopped when I saw the little grains collecting in the newly scrapped pits.

By morning, my tongue had swollen against my lower teeth. There was a dark stain on the pillowcase, and when I tried to drink water, the pain made me spit it back into the sink.

Something hard struck the porcelain.

It was a grain of sand, no larger than the head of a pin. I nearly washed it down the drain before I recognized it.

I picked it up.

I took the grain into the bedroom and placed it in the blue dish.

It looked ridiculous beside the pearls. They were smooth and heavy, with colors that changed depending on the light. The sand was a little piece of rock. I had to move the pearls apart to find it again.

I sat on the edge of the bed for a while. My mouth ached even when I kept it still. I was thirsty, and I felt the emptiness in my stomach. 

Eventually, I picked the grain out of the dish.

I carried it back into the bathroom. I could see my face in the mirror, the swollen jaw and the dried blood at the corner of my mouth. 

I opened as wide as I could, feeling for the place. I sealed my tongue over the grain, feeling full once more.
